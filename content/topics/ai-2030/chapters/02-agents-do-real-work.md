Companies stop piloting agents and start putting them on payroll, in all but name. A typical software team now has more agent "members" than humans, and the humans spend their days reviewing and steering rather than typing.

Entry-level jobs feel it first. Internships in coding, customer support and paralegal work shrink, because the tasks they were built around are exactly what agents do well. The jobs that grow are the ones that need someone to own an outcome: deciding what to build, talking to customers, signing off on risk.

Hacking gets cheaper too. The same agents that patch code can find holes in it, and the first widely reported incidents of agent-run attacks show up in the news.

:::expand[What I'm doing about it]
- Building real projects with agents now, so I'm fluent before it's mandatory
- Learning to judge work, not just do it: reading code, specs and contracts critically
- Keeping one foot in something physical or human, where agents are weakest
:::
